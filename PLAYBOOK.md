### Exterior remodeling appointment setting — Carlsbad / North San Diego County
**Built for: Sam · Tidal Remodeling · 16 knocking hours**

## Read this part first

You asked me to rank things by importance. Here is the ranking, and the honest reasoning behind it.

You have 16 hours of knocking. Software does not make leads — doors make leads. But about two and a half hours of the right preparation multiplies every one of those 240-ish doors, and the wrong preparation costs you the job. So everything in this playbook is ordered by **impact on leads in the next 16 hours, divided by hours to acquire it.** Anything that doesn't clear that bar got pushed to week two or cut entirely.

Three things came out of the research that matter more than anything else in this document, and I want them in front of you before you read another word.

**First: your knocking window is working against you, and it's probably fixable.** You're knocking during company hours. Every source on D2D timing — and I'll be straight, the evidence base here is practitioner consensus rather than hard data — puts the peak residential contact window at 4–8pm, with 3–5pm as the "golden hour" when people arrive home. A 9am–1pm block in a North County commuter neighborhood is close to the worst four hours of the day, because the people who can sign a contract are at work. If you can get your four hours moved to **1–5pm or 2–6pm**, you keep it inside company hours, you overlap the office being open (Tidal is Mon–Fri 9–5), and you catch the golden hour. That single scheduling change is plausibly worth more than everything else in this playbook combined. Ask for it before your next shift. If they say no, you knock 9–1 and you lean much harder on the retiree-and-stay-at-home segment, which is genuinely large in this territory.

**Second: know the actual quota before day four.** You told me "get leads or I'm fired," but not how many. That number decides your whole strategy and you need it today. Here's the benchmark math so you can judge it. Three independent industry sources converge on roughly **2–4 appointments per 100 doors** for non-storm retail exterior work, and coastal San Diego is definitively a non-storm retail market. At a sustainable 15–18 doors an hour, 16 hours is about 240–290 doors, which projects to **5–9 appointments** with solid execution. If your quota is inside that, you're fine and you just need to execute. If your quota is 15 or 20, you are being measured against a number above the industry benchmark, and you should know that now — because it changes what you do. It would mean going all-in on the highest-yield streets, working RepCard follow-up hard to convert "not right now" into later appointments, and having a straight conversation with your manager on day two rather than day five.

**Third: you have an edge in this territory that almost nobody knocking is using.** It's in section 4 and it's the tile underlayment story. Read that section twice.

## 1. The order — what to do, in what sequence

### TIER 0 — Tonight, before your next door (about 2.5 hours)

This is the whole prep list. Nothing here is optional and nothing takes long.

**1. Ask your manager three questions (10 minutes, do it first).** What exactly counts as a lead, and how many do I need? Can I move my block to 1–5pm? And: am I registered as a Home Improvement Salesperson, or am I appointment-setting only? That third one has legal teeth and I explain it in section 7.

**2. Memorize the opener and the close, word for word (30 minutes).** Not the whole script — the first fifteen seconds and the last twenty. You will say these two hundred times this week and you cannot be reading them. Everything in between can flex. These two cannot.

**3. Learn six damage sightings you can call from the sidewalk (45 minutes).** Section 4. Six is enough. You are not learning roofing this week, you are learning to accurately name six things at forty feet.

**4. Learn the eight Tidal facts (20 minutes).** Section 3. These are what make you a person from a real company instead of a stranger. The license number is the single strongest trust artifact you have in California and you should be leading with it.

**5. Set up RepCard properly (30 minutes).** Section 6. Specifically: record the video, and cut your notifications down to five. The default notification set is thirty-plus triggers and it will make the feature useless to you inside a day.

**6. Plan your territory on paper (20 minutes).** Section 5. Drive or street-view your area and mark which streets are the 1990s tile cohort. That's where your best conversation lives.

**7. Get the field app onto your phone properly (10 minutes).** This matters more than it sounds, because it unlocks automatic door counting. Phone browsers only hand out GPS to pages served over `https://`, so a file opened from your downloads will never auto-log. Go to **app.netlify.com/drop** on a laptop, drag the HTML file on, and it gives you a live link. Open that on your phone and **Share → Add to Home Screen**. Then hit *Turn on* under Auto-log doors and allow location. From then on the app logs a door every time you stop at one, and the only thing you tap is **ANSWERED** when somebody opens — which upgrades the door it already logged rather than double-counting it. Calibrate against a hand count on your first block; expect it to get 85–95% of doors, and it's tuned to miss one rather than invent one.

**8. Read your numbers once so you don't panic (5 minutes).** Section 2. On day one you will get rejected sixty times and it will feel like you're failing. You're not — that's what the job looks like when it's going well. Knowing the ratio in advance is the difference between a bad hour and a spiral.

### TIER 1 — During shifts, in priority order

**Volume before polish, on day one.** Your first shift's only goal is doors and reps. Do not stop to optimize. A rough pitch delivered eighty times beats a perfect pitch delivered twenty. You cannot think your way into a good pitch — you talk your way into one.

**Log everything as you go.** Use the field app. The data from day one is what tells you what to fix on day two, and "I think it went okay" is not data.

**Follow up the same day, within two hours.** RepCard's own guidance and every lead-response study point the same direction: speed is the highest-leverage follow-up variable that exists. A text within two hours of the conversation, while they still remember your face.

**One fix per shift. Exactly one.** At the end of each block, look at where the funnel is leaking and change one thing tomorrow. If doors-to-contact is low, that's timing and territory. If contact-to-pitch is low, that's the opener. If pitch-to-appointment is low, that's the close. Fixing one thing well beats fixing four things badly.

### TIER 2 — Evenings, after you've knocked

**Objection responses — but only after you've heard which ones you actually get.** Section 8 has the full list, but don't try to memorize all thirteen tonight. Knock first, note the three objections you actually hear, then drill those three. Everyone's territory serves up a different mix.

**The tile underlayment pitch.** Section 4. Learn it properly on night one or two, once you've seen how many tile roofs are actually in your area.

**The lead form, the QR code, and the CRM.** All built and ready, and all genuinely week-two tools. They only pay off once you have volume flowing through them, and RepCard already does most of this job. Don't spend day-one energy here.

The one exception worth doing early is **connecting the CRM to a Google Sheet**, because that's what makes a homeowner's self-submitted request email the office instantly during business hours and land in a list you can actually work. It's about ten minutes once. After that, the CRM's **NOW** tab answers the only question that matters between shifts — who do I call right now — using the cadence in section 9: two hours, day one, day three, day seven, day fourteen. **Before you use any of it, ask your manager.** Leads are usually company property and Tidal requires RepCard, so treat RepCard as the system of record and this as your own working layer on top of it.

### TIER 3 — Week two and beyond

Video intro refinement, referral asks, review collection, neighborhood social proof, and building out a real re-knock system for the 70% of doors nobody answered. All of it valuable. None of it worth an hour this week.

## 2. Your numbers — what "going well" actually looks like

Print this on the inside of your eyelids. The most common way new canvassers fail is not skill — it's quitting emotionally at hour two of day one because they've misjudged what normal looks like.

| Stage | Realistic range | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Doors per hour | 15–20 sustainable | Below 12 you're overthinking each door |
| Doors that open | 25–35% | Political canvassing field experiments average 29.6% |
| Conversations per hour | 4–7 | This is your real work product |
| Conversations per appointment | 15–25 | Non-storm retail |
| **Appointments per 100 doors** | **2–4** | Three independent sources agree |
| Appointments per 4-hour shift | 1–3 | On a good block, in a good window |

**So a normal, competent, successful day looks like: 65 doors, 20 conversations, 55 doors where nobody was interested or nobody was home, and one or two appointments.** That is not a bad day. That is the job working.

The 29.6% contact rate figure comes from six randomized field experiments published in the *Journal of Politics* — it's the only number in this whole space with real experimental backing. Everything else is trade-press consensus, and I've flagged it as such throughout.

**One critical caveat on the ratios:** every roofing D2D playbook you'll find online is storm-chasing content from Texas, Colorado and the Southeast, built around hail damage and insurance claims. Those sources quote 1-appointment-per-25-doors. **That does not transfer here.** Coastal San Diego has essentially no hail and no storm-restoration market. Use the non-storm number — roughly 1 in 50 — and ignore anyone quoting you Xactimate and adjusters.

## 4. Damage — what you can honestly call from the sidewalk

You are not learning roofing this week. You are learning to accurately name **six things** from forty feet away and say one true sentence about each.

### The one that matters most: the tile underlayment story

**This is your edge and I'd build my week around it.**

The housing stock across Carlsbad, San Marcos, Vista and much of North County is dominated by 1990s and early-2000s tract homes, and in Southern California that cohort was built almost universally with **concrete tile**. Those roofs are now 25–35 years old.

Here's the part nearly every homeowner has wrong, and most canvassers too: **the tile is not the part that wears out.** Concrete tile lasts 50-plus years. The felt underlayment beneath it — the layer that actually stops water once anything gets past the tile — is the wear item, and it's typically rated in the **20–30 year** range, with older organic felt shorter. So there is an entire cohort of homes in your territory sitting on original underlayment at or near end of life, under tile that looks perfect from the street.

> **Say it carefully, for two reasons.** Don't quote an oddly precise band like "twenty to twenty-eight years" — no primary source supports a number that tight, published ranges run *longer* (some California sources say 30+ with quality double underlayment), and a competing roofer will contradict you with a bigger number. Precision you can't back reads as manufactured urgency. And don't say "your tile isn't waterproof" — codes and manufacturers generally treat tile as the primary water-*shedding* layer with underlayment as the secondary barrier, so a competitor gets to say "your guy told you your tile isn't waterproof, that's not how it works." **Anchor on age, which is a fact: this roof is thirty years old and that felt is almost certainly original.** That argument nobody can take from you.

Why this is the best pitch available to you:

It's **real**. It requires zero exaggeration — it's arithmetic on the age of the house. It's **verifiable** — the homeowner can look it up and you'll be right. It's a **better offer** — the fix isn't a tear-off, it's a lift-and-relay where the existing tile is salvaged, new underlayment goes down, and the same tile is reset. That's a genuinely more appealing proposition to present than "you need a new roof." And it's **educational rather than alarming**, which is precisely the register that survives contact with a skeptical homeowner.

The ground-visible tells that support it: cracked or displaced ridge caps at the peak, individual slipped tiles out of course, crumbling mortar at the ridge, and sagging roof planes.

**The tile pitch, as spoken:**

> "That's concrete tile — good roof, honestly. The tile itself will outlast both of us, easy fifty years. What most people don't know is the tile isn't the part that wears out. Underneath it there's a layer of felt, and *that's* the piece with a clock on it — usually somewhere in the twenty-to-thirty-year range. Given when this neighborhood went up, yours is probably still the original. From here I can see a couple of cracked ridge tiles at the peak and one that's slipped out of course over the garage — those are worth dealing with on their own. Whether the felt underneath is done is something I honestly can't tell you from the driveway; somebody has to pull tiles and look. But if it is, the good news is the fix isn't a new roof. It's lifting your existing tile, replacing the felt, and setting the same tile back down."

### The other five sightings

**Missing shingles or tabs.** Rectangular gaps showing black underlayment or a different color, sometimes in a stair-step line. This is a genuine breach of the primary covering and the one condition that honestly justifies "before the rains" language. *Careful:* a shingle that's merely unsealed but intact, not creased or torn, is not damage — the building-envelope institute is explicit about this, and claiming otherwise is how you get caught by a second opinion.

**Cracked or missing ridge caps.** Along the peak. Cap shingles take the most UV and the most thermal movement of anything on the roof, and they sit over a continuous open seam. This looks minor from the ground and is one of the few small-looking things that's genuinely a current water path. High honesty-to-urgency ratio — one of your best findings.

**Granule loss.** The roof reads mottled or uneven from the street, and granules pile up in gutters, at downspout splash zones, and in the planter beds under the eaves. Granules exist to block UV from reaching the asphalt; without them the asphalt ages fast. *The honest boundary:* heavy shedding in year one is normal, a few granules after a hard rain at year ten is normal. What's meaningful is an inch of granules in a gutter run, or large bald areas showing black substrate. **Granules in a gutter on a six-year-old roof is not an emergency, and saying it is will cost you the deal the moment they call a second roofer.**

**Exposed fiberglass mat — the shiny patches.** You listed this and it's a real and useful sighting. What's happening: granules erode, UV hits the asphalt directly, the asphalt hardens and thins, and eventually you're down to the fiberglass mat, which has no UV resistance at all.

Three things about it that most scripts get wrong, and getting them right is what makes you sound like the one honest person who's knocked on that door:

An exposed mat **is not a hole and is usually not a leak.** Asphalt roofs are laid with double or triple coverage — water landing on a bald patch still has to get through the course beneath it and the underlayment. Second, you need to know which of three things you're looking at: small round evenly-scattered pockmarks are *blister craters*, which typically show up in year one, don't leak, and are a ventilation symptom; localized irregular scuffing in traffic paths is cosmetic; but **broad contiguous bald areas across many shingles, worst on the south and west slopes, is true field erosion and that one means end of life.** Third — and this is the honest and more persuasive framing — **the real claim is about repairability, not water.**

> "Once the mat's showing across a slope, that roof has basically stopped being repairable. The shingles get brittle enough that a roofer can't walk it or patch it without breaking more than he fixes. So you're not choosing between repair and replacement anymore — you're choosing when."

No manufacturer or trade body publishes a "years from exposed mat to failure" number. I looked hard. Anyone quoting you one is making it up. Say "a few remaining seasons, not a decade," and label it as an estimate.

**Rot at the fascia and eaves.** Paint blistering or peeling in bands along the fascia, dark grey or black streaking, a fascia line that waves or dips, gutters pulling away at a spot where the wood is also discolored. **This is the best honest finding available to you**, because the eave is the one part of the roof assembly you can see up close and at eye level from the driveway. It's a fact you can point at rather than an inference. Wet rot looks black, soft and spongy with bubbling paint; dry rot looks brown with cracking, splitting and a powdery texture. *The boundary:* you can say "the fascia is soft right here." You cannot say "and therefore your rafters and decking are rotted." That requires probing.

### How to describe what you see so it sounds real

Home inspectors have a professional discipline worth stealing outright. Under their standards of practice, an inspector isn't required to walk the roof, isn't required to predict service life, and is explicitly limited to reporting what was observed. The most credentialed roof observer in the residential market is that careful. Matching that humility from a driveway is what makes people believe you.

Four habits do most of the work.

**Name the thing, its location, and your vantage point.** Not "your roof's in bad shape." Instead: "From right here I can see three or four cracked ridge caps along the peak on this side."

**Grade it plainly — minor, moderate, or extreme.** Inspectors are told to skip the jargon. "Moderate curling on the south slope" lands better than "severe clawing and thermal degradation," because the second one sounds like a sales script and the first sounds like a guy who knows roofs.

**Be definitive about what you observed and hedged about what you concluded.** "The fascia is rotted" — not "appears to be rotted," which reads as uncertainty. But be equally explicit that a conclusion is a conclusion.

**Say the limit of your vantage out loud.** This is the highest-trust move available and it costs you nothing: *"I can't see the north slope from the street, and I can't tell you anything about the decking or the attic from down here."* Volunteering a limitation is the strongest possible signal that you aren't manufacturing a problem. Counterintuitively it makes everything else you said more believable, not less.

## 5. The script

### What's working in your current script

I want to be specific about this, because the bones are good and you should keep them.

**The opener is genuinely strong.** Compliment, then a *specific* reason you stopped, then a question. That's the correct structure and it's better than most of what's published.

**"How long have you owned the home?"** is doing real work — it qualifies for ownership and roof age simultaneously without feeling like qualification.

**"Eventually you're going to get that taken care of, right?"** is the best line in your script. That's a commitment-and-consistency play, and it's clean. Keep it exactly as is.

**The attic sequence is clever.** Asking when they last looked, what they were looking for, what they found — it's a smart way to establish that "no visible leak" doesn't mean "no problem," and it gets the homeowner reasoning alongside you instead of against you.

### What I'd change, and why it makes you more effective

The back half of the script runs a fear chain: moisture is definitely getting in, therefore toxic mold, therefore does anyone have respiratory issues. I'd cut that, and not primarily for ethical reasons — though those exist. I'd cut it because **in your specific territory it's the weakest ground you could stand on, and it's the part that will get you caught.**

Here's why it doesn't hold up in Carlsbad.

Coastal San Diego gets roughly **ten inches of rain a year** — that's the San Diego figure and Carlsbad runs modestly higher, so don't use it to argue rain is negligible up here — with nearly all of it falling between December and March. Wood only decays if it stays above 20–25% moisture content for a sustained period. In a ventilated attic with eight dry months, small leaks typically re-dry long before decay can establish. This is exactly why San Diego roofs commonly run for years with minor active leaks and no structural rot. The "water is rotting your house right now" chain is a Midwest and Southeast script, and it's mostly false here.

On mold specifically: EPA's own framing is allergens and irritants; there are no federal standards for mold; species cannot be identified visually; and mold does not structurally degrade wood — **decay fungi** do, and those are a different organism. And on termites: the **drywood termite** is the most common *drywood* species in coastal Southern California, it nests in dry wood, and it doesn't need moisture at all — UC's integrated pest management program doesn't list roof leaks as a factor for it. (Careful with the phrasing: don't say drywood is "the dominant termite species," because statewide the subterranean termite is the number one pest and any pest-control guy will correct you on the spot. "The most common drywood termite" is the accurate version.) So "your roof leak is causing your termites" is, for the species you're most likely to be talking about, simply not true.

Then there's the legal side, which is not theoretical. California Business & Professions Code **§7161(b)** makes "any substantial misrepresentation in the procurement of a contract for a home improvement" a **misdemeanor**. **§7160** lets a homeowner recover $500 plus attorney's fees from — note the wording — the contractor *or solicitor*, meaning you personally. Telling a homeowner from a driveway that there's mold in their attic, that their decking is rotted, or that their roof is actively leaking is asserting facts you have not observed, and if it induces a sale, that's the statute.

And the practical problem, which honestly might be the biggest one: **CSLB publishes a consumer guide warning homeowners about roofing solicitors, and it specifically warns that scammers "may be well-dressed, clean-cut, polite and personable" and tells people not to be pressured into quick decisions.** Assume some fraction of your doors have read something like it. The mold-and-termites escalation is precisely the pattern they've been told to watch for. It doesn't just risk a citation — it actively trips the scam detector on the exact homeowners who can afford a roof.

The good news is you don't lose anything by cutting it, because the honest version of that same sequence is *more* persuasive.

### The upgraded script

Same structure. Same psychology. Different back half.

**OPENER** — memorize verbatim

> "Morning! Beautiful home. I'm Sam with Tidal Remodeling — we're the roofing company down on Avenida Encinas in Carlsbad. Reason I stopped is I was walking past and noticed **[specific damage, specific location]**. Quick question — how long have you owned the place?"

Three things are happening. The compliment is a small genuine gift. Naming a *specific* thing in a *specific* location is what separates you from every generic canvasser — it proves you actually looked at their house. And the ownership question is an easy yes that starts a conversation instead of a pitch.

**ACKNOWLEDGE + REPEAT THE OBSERVATION**

> "Twelve years — nice. So like I said, the reason I stopped: **[repeat the damage]**."

**EDUCATE — damage plus honest consequence**

Pick the true consequence for what you actually saw. Cracked ridge caps: "The ridge is a continuous open seam and those caps are the only thing covering it — that's a real water path, and it's one of the few things that looks minor from down here but isn't." Exposed mat: "Once the mat's showing, the roof stops being repairable — the shingles get too brittle to patch." Tile: the underlayment story from section 4. Fascia rot: "That's the one part of the roof I can actually see up close from here, and it's soft."

**THE COMMITMENT QUESTION** — keep this exactly

> "Eventually you're going to get that taken care of, right?"

**IF THEY SAY NO / "no leaks" — the honest attic sequence**

> "Good — and that's actually normal. Here's the thing most homeowners don't know: a stain on the ceiling is the *last* thing that happens, not the first. By the time you see it in San Diego, water's usually been getting past the covering for more than a season. When's the last time anybody actually got up there and looked?
>
> Six months ago? What were you looking for — decorations, or damage?
>
> Right. And that's most people. Look, I'm not going to tell you what's up there, because I can't see it from the driveway and neither can anybody else. What I *can* tell you is what I see from here, and that this roof is at the age where somebody should actually look."

That does everything the original did — establishes that no visible leak ≠ no problem, gets them reasoning with you — and the "I'm not going to tell you what's up there, because I can't see it" line is the strongest credibility move in the entire script. It's the moment they stop filing you under salesman.

**THE CLOSE**

> "So here's what I'll do. I'll get one of our guys out here for a free assessment, and you'll get a written number so you've got it for whenever you're ready — this year, in three years, whenever. No obligation, and you're completely free to tell me it's not the right time.
>
> I'm Sam, by the way — what's your name?
>
> *[they answer]*
>
> Good to meet you, **[name]**. I've got tomorrow morning around ten, or Thursday afternoon around four — which one's easier?"

Four deliberate moves in that close. Free assessment with a written number is a low-commitment foot-in-the-door — the same mechanism that took billboard compliance from 17% to 76% in the classic study, though the effect is smaller and more conditional than most trainers claim. "You're completely free to say no" reduces reactance; the published effect sizes on this are contested and probably inflated by publication bias, so don't build your hopes on it, but it costs nothing and it's good manners at a stranger's door. Getting their name *in conversation* and then using it is backed by a real finding — but note the nuance almost nobody teaches: **the effect disappears when it's obvious where you got the name.** Reading it off a mailbox or a lead list kills it and probably reads as creepy. And the two-option close beats an open "when works for you," which invites "let me think about it."

**Say your own name in the opener AND again at the close.** Twice. People genuinely don't retain it the first time.

## 6. Being likable — the mechanics

Likability isn't a personality trait you either have or don't. Most of it is mechanical, and the mechanical parts can be installed in an afternoon.

### Before you say anything

Faces are judged for trustworthiness in about **100 milliseconds** — and judgments made at 100ms correlate highly with judgments made with unlimited time. More looking increases *confidence* in the verdict, not the verdict itself. (The "you have seven seconds to make a first impression" line every sales trainer repeats is folklore from a magazine column, not research. The real finding is worse for you: the verdict is substantially in before you open your mouth.)

So grooming, posture and clothing are doing more work than your opener. Clean branded polo or button-down. Khakis or clean jeans. Broken-in closed-toe shoes. Visible company ID badge at all times. **Never a suit** — it reads as high-pressure sales and creates suspicion. **Never sunglasses** during the conversation, and take the hat off. Park on the street where the logo is visible, never in the driveway.

### Where you stand — highest return per unit of effort

**Stand four to five feet back from the door, not on the mat.** Stand **to the side** of the doorway rather than squared up in the peephole, body angled maybe forty-five degrees toward the street. They see your whole silhouette, the screen door has room to swing, and you are visibly not positioned to push in.

**When the door opens, step back again.** Ceding space at the moment of contact is the strongest nonverbal "I am not a threat" available to you, and almost nobody does it.

Hands visible. Clipboard or phone held low at your side, never raised toward the door.

### While you're talking

**Slow down.** Fast talking is *the* scammer tell. A slow, controlled pace projects confidence and, counterintuitively, gets you interrupted less.

**Plan breaks in eye contact.** Gesture up at the roof, at the ridge line, down the street at the neighbor's house. Sustained unbroken eye contact from a stranger on a porch reads as predatory, not sincere.

**Repeat their exact words back — not a paraphrase.** This is the single most transferable finding in the research. In the study where waitstaff repeated customers' orders back **verbatim** rather than saying "okay," tips went up 68% and tipping frequency went from 61% to 81%. Replicated with a larger sample. So when a homeowner says "we've been thinking about it but the timing's bad," you say **"timing's bad — got it."** Their words. Not "I understand you have some concerns about scheduling."

**Save the self-deprecating joke until after you've proven you know something.** The pratfall effect is real but it's conditional: the competent person who spills coffee is rated *more* likable, and the average person who spills coffee is rated *less* likable. Establish that you know roofs first, then be human. Reversed, it just makes you the guy who fumbles.

**Ask a small favor.** "Mind if I take a look from the side yard?" "Could you point out where you saw the stain?" There's a real if thinly-evidenced effect where doing someone a small favor increases liking for them. Low cost, plausible upside.

**Find the real coincidence.** Shared hometown, same truck, kid the same age, same high school. Trivial shared attributes roughly double compliance in the research. **Do not manufacture one** — fabricated similarity is itself a scammer tell and people are better at catching it than you'd think.

### The two moves nobody else in your territory is making

**Lead with the CSLB license number, unprompted.** "We're Tidal Remodeling, license 984966 — you can look us up at cslb.ca.gov, takes about thirty seconds." Homeowners can verify it. Scammers cannot produce one. In California this is the strongest single trust artifact that exists and virtually no canvasser volunteers it.

**Use RepCard's scannable ID badge as identity verification, not as a business card.** "Scan this — it'll pull up my verification against the company." RepCard fires a notification when someone verifies your badge, which means the feature exists and is built for exactly this. Framing it as *"here's how you check that I am who I say I am"* rather than *"here's my card"* completely inverts the frame you're fighting.

## 7. The legal lines — read once, don't panic

Two things to know and one question to ask your manager.

**Ask your manager: am I registered as a Home Improvement Salesperson, or am I appointment-setting only?** California requires anyone who solicits, sells, negotiates or executes home improvement contracts on behalf of a licensed contractor to register with CSLB as an HIS. There's an exemption — §7152(d)(5) — for "a person who contacts the prospective buyer for the **exclusive purpose** of scheduling appointments for a **registered** home improvement salesperson." Pure appointment-setting is exempt.

But that exemption is narrower than it looks, and both conditions get broken constantly in the field. "Exclusive purpose" means the moment you're quoting price, discussing financing, or handling cost objections at the door, you've probably stepped outside it. Operating as an unregistered salesperson is a misdemeanor under §7153. The registration has **no exam, no experience requirement, no education requirement, and a minimum age of 18** — so if there's any doubt, registering is the cheap fix that ends the argument. Ask, and let your manager sort it out.

**The bright line on what you can say.**

You *can* say: what is literally visible from where you're standing; general material lifespans; roughly how old the roof appears based on style and era; how the local climate ages roofs; that you'd like to get someone out to look; that the assessment is free; who the licensed contractor is and their license number.

You *cannot* say: that the decking, framing, underlayment, insulation or attic are damaged; that mold, rot, or termites are present; that the roof is actively leaking — **any of these when you haven't observed them.** You also can't invent a program, deadline, price, or neighborhood approval that doesn't exist, or imply they have no time to think. The law affirmatively gives them three business days to cancel — **five if they're 65 or older**, which matters a lot in North County demographics.

**Two operational rules.** Any posted "No Soliciting" sign is a hard stop. Gated communities and HOAs across North County enforce their own no-solicitation rules as trespass, and in unincorporated county the code requires you to honor a posted sign and leave when asked.

**Know your city's hours, and know that they differ.** If you're working **Carlsbad**, the rule is Carlsbad Municipal Code §8.34.060: **no soliciting before 8am or after 8pm**, extended to 9pm during daylight saving. The City of San Diego ordinance you'll find quoted all over the internet says 9am to 8pm — that's a *different city*, and SDPD issued an order in 2022 directing officers to stop enforcing it entirely. Don't quote San Diego's rule in Carlsbad; if a homeowner or a cop checks, you're citing the wrong jurisdiction. Your 9-to-5 block is comfortably inside every version of this, but if you ever get an evening block, **8pm is your wall in Carlsbad.**

Also worth knowing: Carlsbad, Oceanside and Vista all require business licenses, and I could not confirm from public records whether any of them require a separate residential solicitor's permit. Tidal almost certainly handles this, but it's worth one question to your manager.

*This is research, not legal advice. Tidal's management should have counsel review any script before it's deployed.*

## 8. Objections

Don't memorize all of these tonight. Knock first, find out which three you actually get, then drill those three.

**"Not interested."** Don't rebut — pivot to a question. *"Totally fair. Has anybody actually been up there in the last few years?"* Then offer a ground-level look, not a commitment.

**"I don't think I have any damage."** *"You might not — and honestly that'd be good news. What I can see from here is [specific thing], which doesn't mean you've got a problem today. It means the roof's at the age where it's worth somebody looking."*

**"We already have a roofer."** *"Good, that's better than most people. When were they last out?"* Then offer a documented second opinion rather than competing.

**"I need to talk to my spouse."** Do not pitch harder. **Book both.** *"Of course — what time works when you're both home?"* Presenting twice kills deals, and a spouse who wasn't there is a guaranteed no.

**"Can you just give me a price?"** This is where you stay inside your lane. *"I'd be guessing from the driveway and you'd be mad at me later. That's what the assessment's for — you'll get a real written number."*

**"How much is the inspection?"** *"Free, and there's no obligation. Genuinely — you'll get a number and you can sit on it for three years if you want."*

**"Are you a storm chaser?"** *"Fair question. No — we're at 5375 Avenida Encinas in Carlsbad, been here since 2013, license 984966. You can look it up right now."* This objection is a gift. It's the one where your facts do all the work.

**"I just had it done."** *"Oh good — how long ago? Who did it?"* If recent, it's a genuine no. Be gracious, ask if they know anyone on the street, leave the card. Referrals from happy homeowners are real.

**"No time right now."** Two specific slots. *"No problem — later this week, or would next Tuesday be easier?"*

**"Just leave me a card."** Give the card — RepCard, tapped or scanned so you get the notification — and then still ask for the two-option close once. If it's still no, leave graciously.

**"You're bothering me."** One line of empathy, offer to leave, leave. *"Totally fair, I'll get out of your hair. Have a good one."* You'll knock this street again in six weeks and you want to be the guy who left politely.

**"No soliciting sign."** Leave. No pitch, no knock. This one isn't a technique question.

**One thing worth knowing about how you book.** There's decent evidence that appointments set through high-pressure or big-ask-then-small-ask techniques carry elevated no-show risk — people say yes to end the conversation. A meta-analysis of 80+ studies found the effect on *verbal agreement* is much stronger than the effect on *actual behavior*. So if a yes felt like it came too easily, over-invest in confirmation: text within two hours, confirm the day before, and use RepCard's calendar reminders. A booked appointment that no-shows counts for nothing.
