Your edge — run this first

1990s tile roof, 25–35 yrs old

The tile isn't the part that wears out. Concrete tile lasts 50+ years. The felt underlayment beneath it is the wear item — typically rated in the 20–30 year range, older organic felt shorter. So the tile looks perfect and the roof is still done.

Look for: cracked or displaced ridge caps at the peak, a tile slipped out of course, crumbling ridge mortar, sagging planes.

"That's concrete tile — good roof, the tile will outlast both of us. What most people don't know is the tile isn't the part that wears out. Underneath it there's a layer of felt, and that's the piece with a clock on it — usually somewhere in the twenty-to-thirty-year range. Given when this neighborhood went up, yours is probably still original. Whether it's done is something I honestly can't tell you from the driveway — somebody has to pull tiles and look. But if it is, the fix isn't a new roof. It's lifting your tile, replacing the felt, setting the same tile back down."
Two phrasing traps. Don't quote a tight band like "20–28 years" — published ranges run longer and a competitor will contradict you with 30+. And don't say "your tile isn't waterproof"; codes treat tile as the primary water-shedding layer. Anchor on age instead — "this roof is thirty years old and that felt is almost certainly original" is a fact nobody can take from you.

The other five sightings

Missing shingles / tabs

Looks like: rectangular gaps showing black underlayment or a different colour, sometimes stair-stepped.

True: a real breach of the primary covering. The one sighting that honestly justifies "before the rains."

"You've got two tabs gone on the west slope — that's the covering actually open, not just worn."
Don't: call a shingle that's merely unsealed but intact "damage." Not creased, folded, torn or missing = not wind damage. A second opinion will catch you.
Cracked / missing ridge caps

Looks like: split, lifted, or missing caps along the peak; a ragged or gapped ridge line.

True: caps take the most UV and thermal movement of anything on the roof and they cover a continuous open seam. Looks minor, genuinely isn't. Best honesty-to-urgency ratio you have.

"From right here I can see three or four cracked ridge caps along the peak. That's the seam at the very top of the roof and those caps are the only thing over it."
Granule loss

Looks like: roof reads mottled from the street; granules in gutters, at downspout splash zones, in planter beds under the eaves.

True: granules exist to block UV from reaching the asphalt. Gone = the asphalt ages fast.

"See the grit in the planter under the downspout? Those are the granules off your shingles. They're the sunscreen — once they're gone the asphalt underneath starts cooking."
Honest boundary: heavy shedding in year one is normal. A few granules after hard rain at year ten is normal. Meaningful = an inch in a gutter run, or bald areas showing black. Granules in a gutter on a 6-year-old roof is not an emergency — saying it is loses you the deal.
Exposed fiberglass mat (the shiny patches)

Looks like: shiny/glassy patches or faint white-grey fibre texture, clearest on shingle edges in low sun.

Know which of three you're seeing:

· Small round evenly-scattered pockmarks = blister craters. Year-one ventilation artifact. Don't leak.
· Localised irregular scuffing in traffic paths = cosmetic.
· Broad contiguous bald areas across many shingles, worst on south/west slopes = true field erosion. This one means end of life.

"Once the mat's showing across a slope, that roof's basically stopped being repairable — the shingles get brittle enough a roofer can't walk it or patch it without breaking more than he fixes. You're not choosing between repair and replacement anymore, you're choosing when."
Don't say: "that's a hole and water's pouring into your decking." Asphalt roofs have double/triple coverage — a bald patch is not a hole. And no manufacturer publishes a years-to-failure number, so don't quote one. "A few seasons, not a decade" — labelled as an estimate.
Fascia & eave rot — your best honest finding

Looks like: paint blistering in bands along the fascia, dark grey/black streaking, a fascia line that waves or dips, gutter pulling away where the wood is also discoloured.

Why it's the best one: it's the only part of the roof assembly you can see up close and at eye level from the driveway. It's a fact you point at, not an inference.

Wet rot = black, soft, spongy, bubbling paint. Dry rot = brown, cracking, splitting, powdery.

"Come look at this with me — right here where the gutter's pulling away, that wood's gone soft. That's the one part of the roof I can actually see up close from the ground."
Boundary: "the fascia is soft here" ✓. "...therefore your rafters and decking are rotted" ✗. That needs probing.

How to say it so it lands

Name the thing, the place, and your vantage. Not "your roof's in bad shape." → "From right here I can see three cracked ridge caps on this side."

Grade it plainly: minor / moderate / extreme. "Moderate curling on the south slope" beats "severe clawing and thermal degradation" — the second sounds like a script.

Definitive about what you saw, hedged about what you concluded.

Say the limit of your vantage out loud. Highest-trust move available, costs nothing: "I can't see the north slope from the street, and I can't tell you anything about the decking or the attic from down here." It makes everything else you said more believable.

Don't memorise all of these. Knock first, find the three you actually get, drill those three.

Where you stand

4–5 feet back from the door, never on the mat. To the side of the doorway, not squared in the peephole, body angled ~45° toward the street.

When it opens, step back again. Strongest "I'm not a threat" signal there is, and almost nobody does it.

Hands visible. Phone/clipboard low at your side. Slow down — fast talking is the scammer tell. Break eye contact deliberately: gesture at the roof, the ridge, down the street.

Repeat their exact words back, not a paraphrase. They say "timing's bad" → you say "timing's bad, got it." Verbatim mirroring is the best-evidenced likability move in the research.

Getting auto-logging working

Phone browsers only give GPS to pages served over https://. A file opened straight from your downloads won't get location — that's a browser rule, not a bug here.

The 60-second fix: go to app.netlify.com/drop on a laptop, drag this HTML file onto the page, and it hands you a live https link. Open that link on your phone, then Share → Add to Home Screen. Now it launches like an app and GPS works.

Then: open it, hit Start shift, hit Turn on under Auto-log doors, and allow location when your phone asks. Choose Always or While using — not "Ask next time."

Two things to know. High-accuracy GPS eats battery — expect roughly 15–25% over a four-hour block, so carry a power bank. And the app has to stay open and in front; if you switch to another app or the screen sleeps, tracking pauses. That's what the screen-awake setting is for. If you do lose it, nothing is lost — just tap doors manually until you're back.

How accurate is it, really? In simulation it catches every door on a clean walk. In the field expect roughly 85–95% — consumer GPS is accurate to about 5–15m and houses here sit 15–25m apart, so it's working near the edge of what the hardware can resolve. It's deliberately tuned to miss a door rather than invent one, because inflated numbers would make you misdiagnose your own funnel.

Calibrate on your first street. Count doors in your head for one block and compare to the auto counter. If it's missing doors, switch to Tight lots. If it's logging phantom ones, switch to Big lots. Watch the panel while you walk — it tells you what it thinks you're doing.

Anything wrong, hit Undo, or tap NO ANSWER to add one it missed. Tapping and auto-logging work together; neither cancels the other.

Your location never leaves the phone. There's no server, no account, nothing uploaded, and no map service contacted. It only lands in the CSV you download yourself.
